Case study · NHL betting models · Nov 2026

NHL Prediction System

Machine-learning models for moneyline, puck line, totals and player props, rebuilt from an audit of two earlier versions and tested the only way that counts in betting: season by season, against sportsbook closing lines.

9,418NHL games, 2019-20 to Nov 2026, labels checked against two sources

6,992games with regulation-result closes, 2021-22 to 2025-26

The short version

Two earlier versions of this project reported a moneyline model that beat the closing line and returned +4.4%. The audit found that 74% of those bets had been graded at prices captured during or after the game. On clean pregame prices the edge disappears, and the +4.4% cannot be reproduced from the saved ledger.

The rebuild starts from audited data, validates every model walk-forward by season, and compares each one with the closing line. One model looked like it had an edge: shots on goal, combined with the market price, returned +8.3% on 907 held-out bets in 2026-27. So I bought 2,260 lines from earlier seasons the model had never been tested on, in two independent batches. The edge did not repeat there (-7.7% on 349 bets). The rule stays frozen and a live test on new games decides it.

The puck line is level with the market, and the other models are sound projections that the market already prices. Adding player tracking and shot-quality data (NHL shift charts and Edge, MoneyPuck game logs) made the player models better forecasters but did not produce a betting edge. Market prices for five earlier seasons showed the game model behind the market in every one, and pinned down why: team ratings, not tie handling. Every result is reported, including the ones that went against the model.

What the data audit found

Both earlier projects were inventoried file by file (143 files in one, 10,819 in the other), large files moved with SHA-256 checks, and every table checked before use.

Critical

Bets graded at in-game prices. About 74% of the first version's bets used prices stamped during or after the game. Those prices score a log loss of 0.29 to 0.46, where a real closing line scores about 0.67. Every result built on them was set aside.

High

Truncated skater data. Three seasons held about 5 skaters per game instead of 36, which fed the prop models' training data. Re-fetched with a fixed sort order and paging; every game now has its full roster.

Trap

A mislabeled prop market. The odds provider's points market is goals, and goals+assists is points. Caught by grading lines against NHL box scores.

Rejected

A public dataset with placeholder odds. A public ESPN-derived dataset lists moneyline −105, total 5.5 and spread 1.5 for every game in 16 of its seasons, often records the underdog's price as the favorite's, and sums its "season" stats across all teams (season wins up to 28,799). It was not used.

Noted

Closing prices that change after the fact. A newer copy of one closing-odds file revised games already played when the provider added a bookmaker: puck-line probabilities moved by up to 0.40 and one spread flipped sides. Both versions' results are kept.

Clean

Game results and labels. Both projects matched the NHL schedule and an independent source on every game, with overtime and shootout handled correctly.

Clean

Goalie logs and the live feed. One starter per team-game in 18,784 of 18,784; the new weekly feed matched the audited tables on all 470 team-games and 940 goalie lines it shares with them.

Method

  1. Pregame features only. Every feature is computed from games dated before the one being predicted. Tests recompute random rows from scratch and compare.
  2. Walk-forward by season. Each test season is predicted by a model trained only on earlier seasons. No k-fold.
  3. Count distributions, not simulation. Shots use a negative binomial; goals use Poisson. Over/under probabilities come straight from the fitted distribution.
  4. Regulation scores with a tie adjustment. Independent Poisson scores predict 16% of games tied after regulation; 21 to 25% are. A tie factor fitted on training seasons fixes it, and moneyline, puck line and totals all come from the same score distribution.
  5. Judge against the close. The market's de-vigged closing probability is the benchmark, and a blend test asks whether the model adds anything the price does not already contain.

Shots on goal, in detail

On its own the shots model is slightly worse than the closing price (0.6821 vs 0.6815 log loss). Its probabilities stray too far from 50%, and betting it alone loses 1.8%.

Combined with the price, it looked like it added information. A blend fitted on 2025-26 lines gave the model a weight of 0.36 (standard error 0.09), and on 2026-27 lines it never saw, the blend beat the market: 0.6764 against 0.6796.

From result to test

That 2026-27 result covered about 260 games of one season, with edge thresholds chosen after seeing the data. Three things followed.

The rule was frozen. Same model, same blend, a 4-point edge, graded only on games after November 6, 2026. Each week the models are retrained and checked against stored fingerprints, so they cannot drift, and the result will be reported after at least 500 bets whatever it shows.

Closing-line value was measured. Between the opening and closing price, the market moves toward the model's opening edge (slope 0.21, standard error 0.007), and more than a model-free recent-form average explains. But taking bets at the open still loses to the fair close on price: the opening margin of about 7% is larger than the move.

The edge was tested where it had never been looked at. The next section has that result.

Both batches disagree with 2026-27, and with each other only within their noise. Combined, the frozen rule loses 7.7%, the model alone loses 2.3% on 1,145 bets, and blending the model in adds nothing to the market's log loss (0.6812 against 0.6809). The gap to 2026-27 is about 16 points, roughly 2.8 standard errors. Either the 2026 result was partly luck, or the model's information was specific to that market and period.

So the shots model is described as having no demonstrated edge. The rule was not changed after seeing this, because changing it would repeat the after-the-fact tuning the frozen test exists to avoid. The live test decides.

What did not work, and why

Points. The model beats a recent-form baseline every season but adds nothing to the closing price on held-out lines. Adding each player's role in the night's lineup (ice-time and power-play rank among dressed teammates, a linemate-quality proxy) improved it in every season, from 0.6551 to 0.6542 log loss, but the closing price still sits at 0.6515. Books know the actual line combinations and same-day news.

Totals. Predicted totals correlate only 0.04 to 0.20 with actual totals, and the model's scoring level lags season-to-season shifts by up to 0.45 goals. Adding a league scoring-level feature did not fix it. Both versions are reported.

Moneyline. 0.6830 against the close's 0.6808 on the full 2025-26 season, better than either earlier version (best 0.6867) but still behind the market, and a blend gives it no weight. Earlier seasons tell the same story (below).

More data, better forecasts, no edge

Three free sources were added as separate model variants, each with its own leakage test, so the frozen live-test models stayed untouched. NHL shift charts for 9,314 games give who played with whom at 5-on-5 and on the power play (ice time from the shifts matches the box score within 30 seconds for 99.9% of skater-games). NHL Edge tracking data is published only as season totals, so each game uses the player's previous-season profile. MoneyPuck game logs add each skater's expected goals, shot attempts, high-danger shots and zone starts, as rolling averages of earlier games.

MoneyPuck improves the shots model in every comparison, including the older seasons it never saw, and real linemates are the most useful single addition for points. Together they close about half of the points model's gap to the closing price. But no variant beats the market on the older lines, and the highest return (Edge, +10.6%) is the best of seven variants tried on the same lines and does not repeat on older ones: the kind of number that selection produces by chance. None of them replaces the frozen rule; the MoneyPuck shots model is tracked alongside it.

Three-way log loss on the regulation result (lower is better), walk-forward model, prices de-vigged (median margin 6.0%). A favorite-only moneyline file for 2021-22 and 2022-23 agrees: the line is ahead by 0.009 and 0.003.

The market is ahead in every season, by 0.0051 overall (standard error 0.0013). A natural suspect was ties, since the model prices more of them than the market. It is not the cause: over five seasons the model's tie rate is right (22.4% against 22.3%; the market's 21.4% is too low), refitting the tie factor to the previous season makes things worse, and even handing the model the market's own tie probability barely helps. The difference is in the home-versus-away split, which means the market rates the teams better. That is where the game model's next improvement has to come from.

Team power rating

An Elo rating built from scratch: margin-of-victory updates, a home-ice term and an off-season pull toward average, with its settings chosen each season from earlier seasons only. It beats a constant every season and a flat Elo in five of six, but trails the game model from 2023-24 on, and adding it to the game model changes log loss by less than the noise. It stays as a power ranking and an optional feature.

Built to be checked

Leakage tests that can fail. Thirteen tests rebuild features from earlier data only and compare them with the stored tables, and three more cover the shift-chart, Edge and MoneyPuck features. They run on every push in GitHub Actions against a generated league with the same files and columns as the real (licensed) data. Planted leaks, team form that includes the current game and goalie features that see the current game, were caught by them; one real leak was found the same way, a traded goalie whose game for his new team started earlier the same evening.

Pregame information only. The game models had been using the goalie who actually started. A probable-starter model, a conditional logit over each team's recent goalies, picks the starter 71% of the time out of sample against 59% for "most starts in the last 10". Swapping it in changed every log loss by at most 0.002, so the look-ahead was small, and the live puck-line rule now uses it.

Every input checked before use. Each odds file offered for the project went through the same checks (official-game matching, score agreement, overlap with sources in use) and was recorded as used or rejected, with the reason.

One command. A fresh machine restores the data with checksums and reruns everything; every committed results file came out byte for byte the same.

Stack

Python, pandas, scikit-learn, statsmodels and SciPy; DuckDB on Parquet; GitHub Actions; Google Drive and Colab for storage and public-API pulls; NHL stats, web, shift-chart and Edge APIs, MoneyPuck, SportsGameOdds and PuckAPI as sources, plus checked third-party closing-odds files for earlier seasons. Every number on this page traces to a script and a results file in the repository, with a decision log and model cards alongside.

Data through November 6, 2026; older-season prop prices from 2023-24 to January 15, 2026; regulation-result prices 2021-22 to October 19, 2026. MoneyPuck data is used under its non-commercial terms. Odds and prop prices are kept out of the public repository.
